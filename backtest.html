    Account & costs

    1 basis point (bp) = 0.01 %. Five bps of slippage on a $100 stock means you pay $100.05 to buy and receive $99.95 to sell. Small, but it is charged on every fill.

    What this teaches

    Look-ahead bias

    A back-test cheats whenever a rule uses information that was not yet known at the moment of the decision. The classic slip: deciding on today's close and then pretending you bought at today's open, hours earlier.

    This engine only ever fills at the next bar's open. Indicators at bar i use bars 0…i only, and the breakout channel excludes the current bar so that a new high cannot "break out" of itself.

    Try it: in the trade list, notice every entry price is an open, one bar after the arrow appears on the signal bar. If a vendor's back-test shows fills at the signal bar's close, ask how.
    Survivorship bias

    The symbols you can type here today are the ones that survived. Companies that went bankrupt, were delisted or got acquired at a loss are not in any watchlist, so a strategy tested only on today's famous names looks better than it would have in real time.

    Apple, Nvidia and Microsoft all look like easy money in hindsight; Enron, Lehman Brothers and Bed Bath & Beyond do not appear in the dropdown at all.

    Try it: run the same rule on five random symbols (the Random button) and on an index ETF such as SPY. If it only works on last decade's winners, it is the winners, not the rule.
    Transaction costs

    Commissions, the bid-ask spread and slippage are small per trade and enormous in total. A rule that trades every few days on 5-minute bars can be profitable with zero costs and lose steadily with realistic ones.

    Costs are charged twice per round trip (entry and exit), and they do not shrink when your edge does.

    Try it: run with 0 bps slippage and $0 commission, then again with 10 bps and $1. Watch the "Costs" tile and the gap to buy-and-hold.
    Overfitting

    Given enough knobs, any rule can be tuned to fit the past perfectly, because it ends up fitting the noise, not a repeatable pattern. The symptom: spectacular in-sample results that collapse on data the parameters never saw.

    Defences: few parameters, many trades, a wide plateau of good settings rather than one spike, and always a held-out out-of-sample period judged only once.

    Try it: press Optimise with 30 % held out. Compare the in-sample and out-of-sample columns of the top row, then look at the plateau chart.
    How to read a back-test honestly

    Ask, in this order: how many trades (fewer than 30 is an anecdote), did it beat buy-and-hold after costs, how deep was the worst drawdown (could you have kept following the rules?), and does it still work on other symbols, other years and slightly different parameters.

    A back-test can only ever show that a rule did not work in the past. It cannot prove that it will work in the future.
